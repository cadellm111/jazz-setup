1. Why it hops — the four real causes, in order

Hopping through a corner on a torsion-beam FWD is almost never a spring-rate problem. Check these in this order, because they cost nothing and each one produces the same symptom.

Check firstYou're riding the bump stops

At 25–75 mm of drop, the front strut is into its bump stop under cornering load. Once the stop is engaged the effective rate goes vertical — the tyre skips instead of following the road, and the car feels like it's hopping on its outside front. Diagnostic: cable-tie test — a zip tie around the damper shaft pushed down to the body; drive; measure how far up the shaft it's been pushed. If it's within 5 mm of the bump stop, that's your answer. Softer damping will not fix it, which is the giveaway.

Check secondNo droop — the inside wheel has nothing to do

If ride height is set by winding the perch down, you get bump travel at the expense of droop. The inside rear then lifts the tyre off the track, the beam axle unloads, and the car snaps and hops. You want roughly 65–70% of the total stroke available in bump, 30–35% in droop — and never less than about 40 mm of droop at the front.

Check thirdRebound set too high

Your dampers adjust rebound only. Too much rebound stops the spring extending between bumps, so the car packs down onto the bump stops over a sequence — the classic "jacking down" hop, and it feels exactly like a car with no suspension. Each click you add is a click you can't take out of compression, because there isn't one.

Check fourthTyres and alignment

R-S4s at road pressures (36+ psi) on a 1,050 kg car will skip and feel vague. Start 28 psi cold all round and aim for 32–34 psi hot; bleed after each session. Zero front toe with -4° camber is stable; toe-out at the rear is not, and the GD3 rear beam has no adjustment until you fit shims.

4. Why 6 / 9 and not 5 / 5

Spring rate at the spring is not what the car feels — wheel rate is, and it's the spring rate multiplied by the motion ratio squared. The rear spring on the beam axle acts well inboard, so it needs a much bigger number to do the same work.

SetupWheel rateRide frequency
Now: 5 kg front (MR ~0.95)4.51 kg/mm1.86 Hz
Target: 6 kg front (MR ~0.95)5.42 kg/mm2.03 Hz
Now: 5 kg rear (MR ~0.70)2.45 kg/mm1.75 Hz
Target: 8 kg rear (MR ~0.70)3.92 kg/mm2.21 Hz
Target: 9 kg rear (MR ~0.70)4.41 kg/mm2.34 Hz

Assumes ~1,050 kg with a 62/38 static split (≈326 kg per front corner, ≈200 kg per rear). Measure your corner weights and motion ratios — the ratios are estimates until you do, and every number above scales with them.

The pointStock is 5/5, which leaves the rear at 1.75 Hz — softer than the front. On a beam axle that means the rear lags every input, the beam steers itself, and the car pushes then snaps. Honed's 6/9 puts the rear above the front (2.0 Hz front / 2.2–2.3 Hz rear), which is what makes a torsion-beam car rotate predictably.

5. Measure first — the order that matters

  1. Ride height. Flat ground, tyre pressures set, half a tank. Measure hub centre to the apex of the guard. Record all four corners. Target 275–290 mm.
  2. Motion ratio. Jack one corner, remove the wheel, put a zip tie on the shaft, refit the wheel, compress the suspension exactly 20 mm at the wheel with a jack and measure shaft movement. MR = shaft travel ÷ wheel travel. Do front and rear — you only need to do this once and it corrects every calculation on this page.
  3. Droop. With the car on stands under the chassis (suspension hanging), measure from the bump stop to the body seat on the shaft. That's your droop travel. Target 30–35% of total stroke, minimum ~40 mm at the front.
  4. Bump stop clearance. Zip-tie on the shaft at the body, drive a hard lap, measure. You want to end up 10–15 mm off the stop — close enough to use it over the kerbs, far enough that it isn't carrying the car.
  5. Corner weights. Only after heights are set. Cross-weight to 50% ± 0.5% if you can, or at minimum get the diagonals within 10 kg.
  6. Alignment last. Ride height changes camber and toe; aligning before the height is final wastes the $150.

6. Bump stops — cut, don't delete

The MPR stops are long, because they're sized for a road car at stock height. At 275–290 mm ride height you're losing real travel to them.

  • Measure the stop first: length, and the shaft diameter it sits on.
  • Cut in 10 mm steps from the bottom (the soft end), never the top. Cut square with a fresh blade; a ragged cut will chew the shaft seal.
  • Re-run the cable-tie test after each cut. Target: stop contact only on the biggest kerbs, not mid-corner.
  • Never remove them entirely. They also protect the damper from bottoming internally, and on a MacPherson strut the stop is what stops the spring going coil-bound.
  • Add a helper/assist spring instead if you find yourself cutting more than ~25 mm — a tender spring keeps the main spring located at full droop and gives a progressive ramp.

WatchCutting the stop increases suspension travel, which lowers the car further at rest if you then wind the perch down to compensate. Trim stops and set height together, not sequentially — otherwise you chase your tail.

7. Preload and droop — the argument that actually matters

MPR advise 7–10 mm of preload. That's a sensible starting point, and it's not the same thing as "preload stiffens the spring" — it doesn't. What preload does is hold the spring against its seats at full droop so it can't rattle or unseat, and it sets how much of the damper's stroke is above and below static ride height.

Method

1. Set the perch so the spring has 7 mm of preload with the car in the air and the spring seated.
2. Drop the car, bounce it, measure ride height.
3. Adjust height only via the lower mount / body, not by adding preload — adding preload to change height is how people end up with no droop.

If you need more droop and you're already at the top of the thread, the answer is a shorter spring, not more preload.

11. Test day — Winton

  1. Baseline session. Pressures 28 cold, dampers 8F/10R, note hot pressures and the highest shaft mark from the zip tie.
  2. Bleed to 32–34 psi hot and re-set. Write it down — that's your starting pressure for every future day at this track in this weather.
  3. Fix travel before damping. If the tie is against the stop, raise the car or cut the stop. Damping changes only after the shaft mark sits 10–15 mm clear.
  4. Then tune one end. Two clicks at a time, one session each, and log it. Two changes at once and you learn nothing.
  5. Alignment check after the car has settled on the new springs — expect camber to shift as ride height changes.
